## Key Stories
- **Meditation in Baghdad**: Signs on the wall said *stressed out? of course you're stressed out, you're in Baghdad* and pointed to a sitting circle or a knitting group. That after-dinner hour became her entry point; she learned the rest from the back pages of Yoga Journal, then went to 3-, 5-, 10-, and 14-day retreats once home.
- **The scholarship that routed her into intelligence**: At the Texas Academy of Math and Science someone told her she seemed smart and should apply. It paid for undergrad against roughly six years of service — a military-style work scholarship, which is how she ended up in government at all.
- **Her mother's downward mobility**: After the Great Recession her mother — who had run a successful back-end call center fixing ATMs — sold samples in grocery stores, stocked a warehouse at night, and sold purses, earning less than Lindsey's graduate stipend while carrying herself as middle class throughout. That became the seed of her research on downward mobility.
- **Driving for Uber herself**: She drove part-time across three years and had research assistants drive in other cities (including a man, to compare experiences), and has interviewed drivers in eight or nine countries over a decade.
- **The doctor with poor bedside manner**: One driver she interviewed was a physician at a prestigious hospital who drove for Uber specifically because his evaluations flagged his bedside manner and he was trying to practice.
- **Eleanor Roosevelt in third grade**: Returning to her K–8 school on the South Side of Chicago after college, a teacher reflected back that Lindsey had spent an entire book report on Eleanor Roosevelt — someone the class had never covered.
- **Her grandmother, born 1909**: Horse carriages in the Deep South until about 1930, and airplane travel later in the same life.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **The mindful moment as a doorway**: In her call-center study, agents took three breaths before answering each incoming call. An acupuncturist she cites used his hand on the doorknob as the trigger. The anchor can be anything repeatable — the point is the threshold.
- **From tool to way of being**: Most practices start as emotional regulation — widening the gap between stimulus and response — and at some point stop being a technique and become integrated. Her sign reads "work is a form of worship."
- **The good bad job**: Gig work and remote work both offer real short-term goods — flexibility, fewer microaggressions, control over earnings. The cost is opacity about the rules and the loss of face time, sticky relationships, and early-career learning that promotion depends on. Research shows introverts, people of color, women, and LGBT people prefer remote work — which is exactly why the tradeoff matters for this audience.
- **You can't ask an algorithm for leniency**: The appeal of not answering to a human boss is real, and so is the loss of the discretion a human manager could exercise.
- **Ancestors as a source of wellness**: Not only blood relations — Lindsey counts Marie Curie, Eleanor Roosevelt, and Ida B. Wells among the lineages she draws on.
- **Build a refuge outside work**: "Your work likely will not love you back." She keeps distinct crews — workout, meditation, gardening — and organizes retreats four or five times a year with them.
- **Small, slow community-building**: Friendship takes many accumulated hours, and she isn't making ten new best friends at a party. Long retreats and structured relating circles (her "art of living relationships" groups) build depth faster than small talk.
- **The rest group**: Two hours, once a week, mid-workday, on Zoom, with an annual in-person retreat. Members take turns facilitating — guided meditation, music, a lecture on the science of rest, or simply being read aloud to.
- **No-input commuting**: She walks or bikes to work with no podcasts and no music. At home, mantras at most; every lamp holds a white bulb and a red bulb, and the house goes red after sundown.
- **Tea ceremony two or three times a week**: Loose leaf, set table, a flower, soft music, sometimes a candle, clockwise pour — a smaller version runs in her office.
