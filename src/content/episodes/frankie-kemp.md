## Key Stories
- **The hallway ambush**: Greg opens with the classic senior-leader-in-the-hallway moment — "What are you working on?" — and the freeze that follows, showing how strong work gets undercut by weak language in seconds.
- **"That's me" in the training room**: Frankie describes running update workshops in tech, architecture, AI, and robotics where participants put their heads in their hands when they see a bad update on the screen, because the waffling, apologizing, and try/hope phrasing is unmistakably their own.
- **The messenger with half the picture**: A leader has to relay a change from senior leadership to their team but only caught fragments in the corridor — Frankie uses this to show how the pressure to reassure while protecting yourself is what pushes people off the rails.
- **From actor to coach**: Frankie draws the line from film and theatre — hierarchical, script-and-director driven, but demanding a performance true to an inner core — to organizational life, where people feel pressured to follow the "organizational playbook" instead of bringing themselves.
- **The dinner table of selves**: Greg and Frankie realize they share the same metaphor — a table of inner voices, only a few of which get called on to speak — and Frankie uses it with clients to ask which voices haven't had airtime lately.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **Swap try/hope for stronger verbs**: Replace "we'll try to make this work" and "hopefully this will succeed" with "our objective is…", "our aim is…", or "we endeavor to…" — language that signals intent without over-promising.
- **Kill the jargon (especially "optimize" and "leverage")**: These non-specific words either mask an unwillingness to go into detail or an attempt to sound like the adult in the room; at senior levels they become a barrier to clarity.
- **Buy time when caught on the hop**: Three of Frankie's 18 techniques — Repeat (echo the question back with considered intonation), Redirect (ask which of your projects they want to hear about), and Comment on the question (talk around it while your headline forms).
- **PEP: Point, Example, Point (or Action)**: The simplest spontaneous-speaking structure — state the headline, give one example, restate the point or name the action. Works for hallway questions, panels, and Q&A.
- **The four-part team update**: What you've previously done → what you're doing now → possible challenges → how you plan to overcome them. Done.
- **What you know / don't know / will find out**: When you're relaying a message but don't have the full picture, this three-part structure is honest, simple, and breeds trust instead of forcing false reassurance.
