## Key Stories
- **The husband who laughed until he cried**: Caverly came home drained from an outward-facing work trip and announced she might be an introvert. Benit's response — that needing an hour or two of quiet after going out into the world does not make you an introvert — opens the episode's whole interrogation of the label.
- **Barnaby dropping the mic**: In the early days of Peace in Schools, Caverly and Sam Hendricks — two "gregarious extroverts" — had gone around for months on how to scale. Barnaby Willett, a deep introvert who'd been quiet through all of it, delivered the perfect summary of where they were stuck *and* named the possibility on the other side of the wall.
- **Her husband as the quiet leader**: Benit found work where he's valued for backbone service rather than volume, after years of struggling to find a role where an introvert could be seen. Caverly watches him drive a cultural shift in the nonprofit without ever being the loudest person there.
- **Greg's 1,500-person audience**: Greg loves public speaking, has done it for 15–20 years, and still gets a full-body anxiety response every single time — the setup for Caverly's answer that practice doesn't delete your wiring.
- **The negative self-talk exercise**: On retreats and in Peace in Schools classrooms, people reveal their inner critic out loud. Caverly has never met anyone not engaged in it.
- **Greg's parents didn't talk to him that way**: Greg notes the inner voice can't simply be an inherited parental voice — his parent never spoke to him like that, but likely had the same voice in their own head. This is the episode's cleanest illustration of collective conditioning.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **SNAP** (also taught to teenagers through Peace in Schools): **S**ee it — notice the conditioning arising. **N**ame it — "I'm believing the voice of the inner critic." **A**llow/accept — embrace what's here without futzing with it, fixing it, or changing it. **P**resence — return to what you actually are.
- **Personal vs. collective conditioning**: Personal conditioning is the people-pleasing and get-it-right-to-earn-love wiring. Collective conditioning is the cultural water we swim in — including the bias toward extroversion. Practices that address only the personal layer leave the bigger half untouched.
- **The anxious part rides in the car, but doesn't drive**: Rather than eliminating anxiety, relocate it — you're the driver, the frightened part is in the back seat receiving unconditionally loving reassurance.
- **Meet resistance with curiosity, not impatience**: When you can't get past "allow," treat the part that hates what's happening as a young part with valid reasons. "Tell me more about why this is so terrible" instead of "can you get over this already."
- **Sky and clouds**: If awareness is the sky, imposter syndrome and its whole weather system of negative self-talk are passing clouds. Returning to presence means remembering you're the sky.
- **Presence ≠ the present moment**: The present moment is where you aim your attention. Presence is recognizing you are not what you're habituated to think you are — even time is something happening *inside* presence.
- **The hungry ghost**: The ego is a ghost with a tiny mouth and an enormous belly, permanently unsatisfiable. Its hobby is seeking and resisting, all day long. You can't fix the content your way out of it.
- **Practice without the self-improvement distortion**: Keep the breathing practice before the talk — just drop the belief that you're the flawed one being repaired by it.
- **Mindful minutes**: You don't need thirty minutes on a cushion. If you scroll three times a day, make one of those doing nothing. Train it the way you'd train for a marathon.
- **The foster-dad test for self-compassion**: If a six-year-old were dropped on your doorstep feeling bad, you wouldn't distract yourself away from his discomfort — you'd sit with him. Avoidance is a failure to allow.
