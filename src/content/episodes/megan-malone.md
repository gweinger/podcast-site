## Key Stories
- **Finding out at 20 that she was an introvert**: Megan took the Myers-Briggs in a junior-year psychology course and had her first language for something she'd always experienced, kicking off what she calls a decade-long process of learning her own limits.
- **Knowing more than the executives in the room**: Working alongside people with 10, 20, and 30 more years of experience, she assumed she had nothing to offer — then realized that in her own areas she often knew as much or more, and that leaning into that was what unlocked her willingness to speak up.
- **Answering Slack from her therapist's office**: Her boss messaged at 6 p.m. while she was mid-session, and she paused the conversation to reply. Catching herself doing it became the moment she decided she needed real boundaries with work.
- **The people-pleaser's promotion trap**: Early in her career she took on everything to prove herself, and found the reward was not recognition but permanently higher expectations than her peers carried — more stress and more burnout for a marginal raise.
- **Greg layering in what he wasn't good at**: Greg describes building his career on strong one-on-one relationships across a company, then deliberately adding group-setting participation later as a complementary skill rather than a fix for a deficiency.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **Self-awareness as the unlock**: Personality assessments (Myers-Briggs, DiSC, Big Five, Enneagram) aren't oracles and won't tell you everything — they give you enough structure to notice repeated patterns and ask whether the path you're on is actually authentic to you.
- **Build on strengths, stay aware of blind spots**: Grow primarily where you're already strong, but don't go blind to your challenges. If a role forces you to work only against your strengths and never with them, treat that as a signal about the role, not about you.
- **"Let me get back to you" as a power move**: Claiming processing time reads as thoughtful, not weak. Most people prefer a considered answer later to an improvised one now — Megan's phrase for the alternative is "jazz hands."
- **Boundaries as energy protection**: Name your working hours, stop responding after them, and communicate the boundary explicitly rather than hoping people infer it.
- **Prepare for conflict like an introvert**: Before a hard conversation, work out what you want from it, the key points you must land, and the other person's likely perspective — preparation is what keeps you from reacting in a way you'll regret.
- **I-statements, the ten-second pause, and two-way openness**: Say "I feel frustrated when" instead of assigning blame; step away briefly when heat rises; stay genuinely open to feedback without sliding back into people-pleasing.
- **Visibility through teaching**: Rather than performing extroverted self-promotion, get visible by educating people on what you already know deeply — the format changes, the depth is the asset.
