## Key Stories
- **The Barnaby "mic drop" moment**: During exhausting, months-long meetings to figure out how to scale the nonprofit Peace in Schools, Caverly's quietest colleague, Barnaby Willett, cut through the circular debate with a single crystallizing observation that named both what was blinding the team and the way forward — a live example of the wisdom that collective conditioning trains us to overlook in introverts.
- **Naming collective conditioning**: Caverly draws a line between personal conditioning (people-pleasing, needing to get things right for love) and collective conditioning — the cultural bias that rewards raised hands in school, gives better grades to the loudest kids, and later passes over quietly high-performing employees for promotions and raises in favor of people who think and talk out loud.
- **The savage inner voice exercise**: In her retreats and in Peace in Schools sessions with teenagers, Caverly has participants reveal their harshest negative self-talk out loud — and every time, the room recognizes the same brutality in each other's inner monologue, proof that the voice tearing you down isn't uniquely yours, it's collective.
- **The foster-dad metaphor**: To illustrate self-compassion, Caverly asks Greg to imagine a six-year-old foster child showing up scared on his doorstep — you wouldn't tell him to get over it or distract yourself from his pain, you'd sit on the couch with him. That's the relationship she says we owe the anxious, imposter-feeling part of ourselves.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **The SNAP method**: A four-step practice for the moment the inner critic spikes. See it (notice the reaction arising — sweaty palms, the voice saying "what the hell are you doing here"). Name it ("the inner critic is present"). Allow it (stop fussing with it or trying to fix it — just let it be here). Return to Presence. The goal isn't evicting the anxious part of you; it's moving from being driven by that part to being the driver, with the anxious part riding along in the backseat, reassured.
- **Two doorways out of imposter syndrome**: First, recognize and befriend the anxious part of yourself through mindfulness and self-compassion practice — treat it the way you'd treat someone you love. Second, and more radical, question whether you are even the part of you that has imposter syndrome at all — Caverly's invitation to stop identifying with the anxious self entirely rather than just managing it.
- **The sky-and-weather image**: Caverly's core model for "presence" — if your being is an open, aware sky, then anxiety, imposter syndrome, and negative self-talk are just weather passing through it. Returning to presence means remembering you are the sky, not the storm currently moving across it.
- **Talk to yourself like you'd talk to someone you love**: Caverly's self-compassion test — most people say things to themselves in anxious moments they would never say to a stranger, let alone someone they love. Noticing that gap is itself a doorway to a kinder internal voice, and the practice of offering that same reassurance to yourself does double duty: it calms the anxious part and reminds you that you are also the part capable of offering the reassurance.
