## Key Stories
- **The executive-presence feedback**: Early at Accenture, Kacie kept hearing she needed more executive presence and to "speak up more." Instead of overhauling her personality, she volunteered to onboard one or two teammates at a time, then graduated to lunch-and-learn sessions where she was already the subject-matter expert.
- **Becoming the go-to person**: When she asked her manager how to get promoted, the answer was to become the person the team went to when things broke. She used that as the foundation for taking real ownership before ever pursuing a formal leadership title.
- **Leading the Charity Committee**: On the Accenture Charity Committee she led a team of five analysts, ran meetings, delegated tasks, and interacted with executives — practicing leadership skills entirely outside her project.
- **Taking over an existing cutover project**: Dropped in as manager of a legacy-to-new-system conversion spanning up to 15 teams, she established credibility by reading the docs, questioning her predecessor, shadowing team members in one-on-ones, and staying humble rather than arriving with fixed ideas.
- **Earning a Chilean client's trust in Spanish**: As a scrum master in Chile, she couldn't connect with the product owner until she started asking pointed questions about edge cases and technical considerations during requirements sessions — questions that quietly displayed her expertise without putting her on the spot.
- **The junior developer's one-on-one**: In a 30-every-30 with her most junior developer in Chile, he admitted he didn't understand the value of what the team was delivering. That single comment led her to have the product owner explain the business value at the start of each sprint, boosting the whole team's motivation.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **The onboarding-to-lunch-and-learn ladder**: Practice speaking up by first walking one or two new hires through the application, then presenting a topic to your peers at a lunch and learn. Low pressure, familiar material, and it builds the reps you need before higher-stakes settings.
- **Informal leadership before formal leadership**: Join a committee, ERG, or volunteer to facilitate a recurring meeting (like a ticket status meeting). You get real experience leading meetings, delegating, and speaking to senior leaders without waiting for a promotion.
- **Joining an existing project — three moves**: (1) Get up to speed fast by devouring the documentation, (2) ask the person you're replacing every question you can to fill the gaps, (3) schedule one-on-ones and shadow each team member so you understand what they own before you try to change anything.
- **Thoughtful questions as the introvert's spotlight-flip**: In client meetings or in a second language, lead with questions about edge cases and technical considerations. The client does most of the talking, but your expertise shows through the quality of what you ask.
- **30-every-30 one-on-ones**: Use short, regular one-on-ones as a lower-stakes place to surface ideas and concerns before raising them to the wider group — and as a channel for junior team members to flag things leadership has stopped seeing.
- **Evidence-based imposter check**: When anxiety spikes before a presentation, ask two questions: "Is there hard evidence I should be feeling this way?" and "Have I done something like this before?" Ground the fear in facts instead of worst-case scenarios.
- **One-thing-per-meeting rule**: Set a goal of saying one thing in every meeting you attend. Each rep becomes proof you can point back to the next time your inner voice says you can't.
