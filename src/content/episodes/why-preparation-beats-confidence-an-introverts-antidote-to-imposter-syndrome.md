## Key Stories
- **The "New York, New York" ambush**: Pitching a comedy series to a room of five aggressive writers, Lee mentioned he was from New York — and the whole room broke into singing "New York, New York" to throw him off his game. It didn't go well, and Lee uses it as a clear example of what happens when a hostile room catches an underprepared introvert off guard.
- **Containers for extroversion**: Lee's technique for making extroverted situations survivable is to assign himself a defined role or limit — a party becomes bearable with a one-hour time cap, a conference becomes bearable if he has "a job," like giving the keynote or sitting on a panel. The container, not raw willpower, does the heavy lifting.
- **Sneaking into the room early**: Before a keynote, Lee arrives ahead of time to stand in the exact spot he'll speak from, locate the outlets, check the screen — and just absorb the physical feel of the space. Treating the unknowns of a room as something to rehearse away, not something to fear, is central to how he beats performance anxiety.
- **The waffle-iron origin story**: Lee's go-to teaching example for storytelling is Nike's founding myth — a co-founder using a waffle iron to press the first shoe sole because he believed running could make the world healthier. He uses it to show how a bold, specific origin story connects an audience far more than a résumé of accomplishments.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **Preparation as the antidote to imposter syndrome**: Rather than trying to manufacture confidence, Lee over-prepares until he has "a lot of tricks in the bag" that make him unstumpable — the sense of readiness itself displaces the feeling that he doesn't belong in the room.
- **Going "off book"**: Borrowed from acting — memorize the material deeply enough that you can set the script aside and speak freely. Lee treats this as the real test of expertise: if you can't say it without reading it, you don't know it as well as you think.
- **Index cards and signposts, not scripts**: Instead of memorizing every line, Lee writes only the first line of each section on a numbered index card so he can navigate from signpost to signpost and improvise the rest without losing his place or his train of thought.
- **Trying on a persona (the "Dharma suit")**: In hostile pitch environments, Lee consciously plays a role — an easygoing "story salesman" — as a deliberate, temporary costume rather than a permanent change to who he is. Extreme cases call for extreme measures; you don't have to become the persona, just borrow it for the room.
- **Read the room, then let it read you back**: Borrowing a playwright's habit of sitting in the back of the house during rehearsals, Lee watches how an audience actually responds in real time — laughter, restlessness, silence — and adjusts pacing and delivery accordingly instead of pushing through a fixed script.
