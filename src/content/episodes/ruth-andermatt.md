## Key Stories
- **The CEO who placed one question**: A CEO who ended up in the top job in a male-dominated industry stopped copying the bold, outspoken people around her. In board meetings she asked "Have we considered...?", held steady without rushing to fill the silence, and watched the idea ripple through the room.
- **The admin manager who couldn't be replaced**: When Ruth was in corporate, a branch manager wanted to fire his admin manager, but there was no budget to backfill the role. He chose to have the hard conversations instead. Six months later, he and his wife were having dinner with her and her husband.
- **"But I'm not the tallest in my family"**: At an offsite, Ruth told a bright, charismatic 6'2" CEO in his 60s that he was tall. He answered that he wasn't the tallest in his family, a childhood "not enough" pattern still running the show in a room full of shorter people.
- **The red construction paper door**: During COVID, an executive working from home with kids aged about four and seven taped red paper over her office door to mean "emergencies only." She later used the same closed-door/open-door rule with her staff of 25. Now a CCO, she says clear boundaries made everyone more productive.
- **The coach who said "good enough"**: After 28 years of coaching others, Ruth hired a business coach who called her on her own overthinking. He pushed her to stop perfecting her files of notes and put her work into the world.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **Coming home to your power**: Before you walk into a room you're leading, ground yourself on purpose with a breath, your feet on the floor, a walk, or a few minutes without your phone. It isn't a destination. It's a returning point you come back to whenever you get distracted. Keep a pad of paper nearby to capture stray thoughts so they don't take over.
- **Place the question, then hold steady**: You don't need volume to influence a meeting. Ask a well-framed question such as "Have we considered...?" and resist the urge to fill the silence. Let the room work with it.
- **Gentle nos and consistent boundaries**: Boundaries usually fail quietly. You only notice when anger or resentment shows up. Start with small, simple boundaries you can succeed at, decide what "no" looks like, and then act on it every time. Saying no and then making an exception teaches people your boundary doesn't mean anything.
- **Balancing masculine and feminine leadership energy (not gender)**: Combine the "masculine" side (goals, KPIs, measurement, direction) with the "feminine" side (collaboration, community, conscious conflict). Introverted leaders tend to listen well. They also need to recognize when the discussion is done and say, "Now this is what we'll do." Ruth describes the leader as the conductor who decides when it's time for conversation and when it's time for a decision.
- **Kind over nice, and treat silence as a warning sign**: Being nice avoids friction, and Ruth says somebody always gets hurt. Being kind has boundaries and speaks up. Healthy disagreement shows that people are engaged. A team going silent is the real danger, because it can signal withdrawal rather than agreement.
