## Key Stories
- **Choosing writing over the saxophone**: Back in New York after college with his sax and ready to gig, Lee watched session musician friends sight-read and understood in that moment he'd never be professional. The writing, he could do — and he started getting plays produced.
- **Taking novels apart with an engineer's mind**: He describes dismantling Nabokov and others to understand mechanically how a story works, short of the retype-all-of-Hemingway extreme.
- **The "New York, New York" pitch**: Five aggressive guys in a comedy pitch asked where he was from, then broke into song when he said New York. It threw him completely off his game and the pitch went nowhere — the episode's clearest picture of a hostile room.
- **Knocking on a Kentucky radio station door**: Needing a correspondent's voiceover recorded same-day for that night's air, Lee simply found a radio station, knocked, and asked to borrow the booth. His example of scenario-building paying off.
- **The high school short story read aloud**: A teacher read his assignment to the class and people laughed at the funny parts — the first time he understood writing could affect other people, and now the personal story he uses to open talks.
- **Reporting for the Fort Worth Star-Telegram**: A college internship required walking up to total strangers and saying "I'm from the newspaper, tell me what happened." The role itself was the container that made it possible.
- **Greg as host rather than guest**: Greg notes he was always far more comfortable at a party he was hosting, and now volunteers to run meetings for the same reason.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **Containers for extroversion**: Set the terms before you walk in — an hour at the party with permission to leave, or a defined job (give the keynote, sit on the panel, sell the show). A role converts an open-ended social demand into something bounded.
- **Preparation as the antidote to imposter syndrome**: "I know I'm pretty good at this" is earned by knowing the material cold. Over-preparation is explicitly permitted — print every transcript, have an AI summarize your main points, whatever reinforces the evidence of your expertise.
- **Get out from between your ears**: Record yourself and listen while walking. Then present it to an actual human — spouse, friend, coworker — to move from reading text to connecting with a person.
- **Being off book**: Borrowed from acting. The real test of whether you belong in the room is whether you can speak it without the paper. Memorize signposts, not sentences.
- **Tech run in the real space**: Sneak into the room beforehand, stand where you'll stand, find the plug, imagine eye contact. Normalizing the space defuses performance anxiety.
- **Memory palace, any geography**: Associate ideas with trees in a garden — or kitchen cabinets — then replay the walk as you speak. Lee confirms the classical technique works and travels.
- **Read the room like a playwright**: Sit in the dark at early rehearsals and listen. Laughter, fidgeting, candy wrappers, actors stumbling — an actor tripping on lines is usually a writing problem, not an acting one.
- **Story gap structure for pitches**: Make the bold statement first ("the pharmacy system is broken"), then reveal the fix. Leading with the solution kills the drama and the Netflix rom-com would be two minutes long.
- **Origin stories**: Nike's power wasn't the waffle-iron soles, it was Phil Knight believing that if people ran, the world would be better. Find the belief, not just the innovation.
- **Try on the Dharma suit**: In hostile rooms, take on a role — "story salesman" — as a deliberate persona. You don't have to become that person permanently.
- **Only you know you dropped a line**: The audience never knows what you left out, and you'll usually fold it back in later anyway.
