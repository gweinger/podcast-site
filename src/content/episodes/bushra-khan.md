## Key Stories
- **The engineering students who wanted her to send the email**: Her undergrad and master's engineering students ask her to assign their groups and to email teammates who have gone quiet on a project. Her answer — "I don't know. What do you do?" — is the beginning of the coaching, not a dodge.
- **The MBA group riot**: Her MBA students clock their preferred partners on day one; try to reshuffle groups six or twelve months in and it's "an absolute riot." The contrast matters: the engineering students who start lower on confidence and grit surpass their own starting point far more dramatically in four months.
- **Manufacturing hardship on purpose**: With AI absorbing the low-hanging difficulties — scheduling, awkward email replies, brainstorming — she deliberately engineers real-time conflict and uncomfortable conversations into her courses. Students dislike her for the first two weeks and give her strong reviews by semester's end.
- **The four rounds of role-play**: An MBA student said she simply couldn't speak to a classmate. Bushra played the classmate, over the top, and made her interrupt. First round: laughing. Second: less giggling. Third: "Oh my God, Bushra's not gonna let me go until I do this." Fourth: she leaned in — mirrored, validated, empathized, and named her concerns, in her own register, not Bushra's.
- **The number-two who held the room**: Greg's story about a senior leader at a former company who was notoriously quiet, completely comfortable with silence, and whose words carried enormous weight — sometimes by naming an uncomfortable truth nobody else would.
- **The styrofoam wall**: The brilliant, highly specialized technical leaders she coaches keep hitting the same barrier — "we already have the solution, why aren't they just listening?" — because they haven't worked out what's stopping the other person.

## Techniques & Frameworks
- **The three-question notebook check (for whoever is facilitating)**: Walk in with a notebook and pen. One — did you get a full sentence from every person, not a "yep, I agree"? Write each name down as you hear them. Two — did everyone offer a plus (what they appreciated), a delta (what they'd change, *with a suggested solution*, so you don't open Pandora's box), or an aha (what made them pause)? Three — if someone stayed silent, follow up within 24 hours: "Hey, you were really quiet. Can we go for a coffee?" You only need to do this once or twice; after that the room has learned you're a safe person.
- **MVE — mirror, validate, empathize**: Mirror the concern back in the other person's own words, then always ask "Did I get that right?" or "Is there more?" — possibly several times, because a person gathering their thoughts rarely delivers them neatly the first time. Validate why the concern is genuinely hard. Then empathize, which is not agreement and not niceness: name the position they're in. Most conflict isn't about content, it's about "you're not listening to me," and you can watch the shoulders drop when that signal lands.
- **Rehearse the interruption**: Ask when the person was last steamrolled and what that looked like, then role-play it on the spot — how to interrupt professionally, confidently, and authentically. It feels silly for the first few rounds; that's the mechanism. If someone tells you their voice isn't being heard, role-play with them rather than advising them.
- **The body-language cue as a low-cost signal**: Elbows on the table, hands clasped, shoulders back, body leaning forward — the opposite of crumpling into your seat behind a notebook. Even without saying a word, it prompts others to ask whether you had something to add.
- **The two-minute power pose before a high-stakes meeting**: Pick a ridiculous pose — flexing, Superman, one arm up — and hold it for two full minutes, ideally in front of another person, not just a mirror. She makes MBA capstone teams do it before presenting to hundreds of people: the giggling stops after 20–30 seconds, the chest opens, and you walk in having already laughed at yourself.
- **Two mentors, one question set**: Find a mentor you'd like to resemble in ten or fifteen years and one who's roughly where you are now. Ask both the same question — "how did you get to where you are today?" — write down the answers, and look for the similarities and differences. That's your data collection, and it becomes the work plan for the next promotion or project.
